BackgroundContext and objective

Contents: problem context, survey design, variables and research questions.

Problem context

Students’ mathematics achievement varies substantially across schools: in some schools most students score high, in others many still struggle to meet the competency standards.

The institution wants to know why. Is the variation mainly due to individual student characteristics (prior ability, socioeconomic status, study time), or also to the school environment (facility quality, teacher experience)?

Students in the same school share facilities, academic environment and teachers, so their scores tend to be similar and are not independent. Treating all students as independent would ignore school information.

Survey design

A survey randomly selected 30 schools, then 30 students from each (900 students). The data have a two-level hierarchy: students nested in schools.

LevelVariableDescription
1 · Studentmath_score (Y)Mathematics test score
1 · Studentprior_scoreMathematical ability before instruction
1 · StudentsesSocioeconomic conditions of the family
1 · Studentstudy_hoursAverage weekly hours studying mathematics
1 · StudentgenderMale / Female
2 · SchoolfacilityQuality of learning facilities
2 · Schoolteacher_expMathematics teachers’ years of experience

Research questions

  1. How much variation is attributable to differences between students, and how much to differences between schools?
  2. Can student characteristics explain differences in achievement?
  3. Can school characteristics explain differences across schools?
  4. After accounting for both, do between-school differences remain unexplained?

Why a multilevel model?

Intraclass correlation violates the independence assumption of OLS: standard errors of school-level variables become too small. Here ICC = 0.082 and the design effect = 1 + (30 − 1)·ICC = 3.38.

Method: linear mixed models with a random school intercept (REML for estimation, ML for LRT/AIC/BIC). See the Models tab for M1–M4.

Analysis workflow

  1. EDA and data checks
  2. Null model → ICC (Q1)
  3. M2 student predictors (Q2)
  4. M3 school predictors + robustness (Q3)
  5. Residual τ₀₀ test (Q4)
  6. M4 random slope, diagnostics, conclusions

Objective

To help the institution decide whether interventions should be directed primarily toward individual students, the school environment, or both. Use the tabs above: Overview for a quick picture, Models to understand each model, and the Q1–Q4 tabs for each question.

Model 1: Null (unconditional) model

What it is. A model with only an intercept and a random school intercept. It has no predictors; every school gets its own average score u₀ⱼ around the grand mean γ₀₀.

Why we fit it. To split the total score variance into a between-school part (τ₀₀) and a within-school, between-student part (σ²). Their ratio is the ICC and shows whether a multilevel model is needed at all.

Yij = γ00 + u0j + eij
u0j ~ N(0, τ00), eij ~ N(0, σ²)

Result. γ₀₀ = 77.79, τ₀₀ = 3.165, σ² = 35.408, ICC = 0.082, design effect = 3.38. About 8.2% of variation is between schools.

Model 2: Student-level predictors

What it is. Model 1 plus the four student characteristics as fixed effects (prior_score, ses, study_hours, gender). The intercept is still allowed to vary by school.

Why we fit it. To test whether individual traits explain score differences (Question 2). Comparing σ² with Model 1 shows how much within-school variance they explain.

Yij = γ00 + γ10·priorij + γ20·sesij + γ30·studyij + γ40·maleij + u0j + eij

Result. σ² falls 35.41 → 0.507 (98.6% explained); τ₀₀ falls 26.1%. All four predictors are significant; SES is the strongest.

Model 4: Random slope for study_hours

What it is. Model 3 where the effect of study hours is also allowed to differ between schools, via a random slope u₃ⱼ that is correlated with the random intercept.

Why we fit it. Model 3 fails the homoscedasticity check across schools. The random slope absorbs the extra school-specific spread and tests whether study hours work equally well everywhere.

Yij = γ00 + … + (γ30 + u3j)·studyij + u0j + eij
(u0j, u3j) ~ N(0, T), T = [τ00, τ03; τ03, τ33]

Result. LRT vs Model 3 = 265.8 (p < 0.001); slope SD = 0.76, ρ = 0.81; σ² falls to 0.354. Homoscedasticity is restored.

Conclusions by question

  1. Q1. ICC = 8.2%: variation is mostly between students (91.8%), but schools remain real (p < 0.001).
  2. Q2. Student characteristics explain 98.6% of within-school variance; SES is strongest, then prior ability.
  3. Q3. teacher_exp is robust (4/4 specifications); facility is positive but inconsistent (1/4). τ₀₀ falls another 16.3%.
  4. Q4. Between-school differences remain (62% of the initial τ₀₀; p < 0.001).

R² marginal and conditional (Nakagawa)

Fixed effects explain nearly all variation in scores.

Intervention recommendations

  • Student level (priority): SES and prior ability, e.g. remedial tutoring and socioeconomic support.
  • School level: improve teacher quality/experience (robust); facilities are positive but not consistent.
  • Study hours: the effect depends on the school context; tailor programmes per school.
  • Combined approach: between-school variance remains; investigate other school factors.

Limitations

  • Observational data: associations, not causation.
  • Only 30 schools: limited power at level 2, and effects are sensitive to particular schools (see leave-one-school-out).
  • Normality and linearity hold; homoscedasticity across schools holds only after adding the random study_hours slope (M4).
  • M4 inference relies on the intercept–slope correlation (ρ = 0.81) estimated from 30 schools.
